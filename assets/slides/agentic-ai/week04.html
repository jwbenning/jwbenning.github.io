Something to think about

Would a project-centred follow-on to this course be useful next semester? Each person brings a project of their own and builds it with an agent over the term.

What we asked

One prompt, your topic

Four lines to fill in (topic, scope, cutoff date, Chrome yes/no), then the agent works alone.

  1. Plan: Opus splits the topic into up to 5 sub-questions.
  2. Search: one Sonnet subagent per sub-question searches OpenAlex in parallel, reads at most 20 abstracts, and reads open-access full text. About 25 papers kept in all.
  3. Full text via Chrome (optional): up to 10 more papers read through the Cornell library.
  4. Evidence table and draft: one row per paper, with design and strength of evidence. Opus writes a ~3,000-word review: what the evidence shows, where people disagree, what would settle it, ways forward.
  5. Critic: a fresh Opus subagent reviews it as an Annual Reviews editor would; Opus revises once.
  6. Check and publish: every DOI looked up at Crossref; the review goes up as a page, with an untouched copy kept as review_original.html in case you revise it later.
Deliberately left out

The name of the review you compared against. Naming it invites the model to recite it.

Small groups · 20 minutes

How did your review do?

Mix people who ran it with people who did not. Walk through one review together.

  1. What did it miss?
  2. Are the references real, and do they say what the review says?
  3. Is the disagreement section real, or balance it made up?
  4. Does it judge evidence, or count it?
  5. Would this be useful to you?

Whole class · 15 minutes

Where is this helpful, and where is it harmful?

  • Where would this speed up real work: a first pass at a new field, a reading list, a check on your own review?
  • Where could it do damage, and how might one guard against that?
  • Did reading full text instead of abstracts change anything you could see?

The test

A test we can score

Adaptation at range edges, against Angert, Bontrager & Ågren 2020 (Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics).

  • Only literature published by 31 August 2020, the newest thing the review cites.
  • A rubric written from the review before any run: 20 points it makes, 5 on how critical it is, plus citation checks.
  • Two runs: a light one on an earlier draft of the class prompt (Chrome off), and a heavier version. Next slide: exactly what differed.

What the checks caught
  • Its first-draft reference list was typed from memory, with at least one error. The Crossref step fixed it.
  • It called one study "the one direct test" of gene swamping. It never found the stronger test Angert cites (Fedorka et al. 2012).
  • It mentioned that an edge on the map is not necessarily an edge in climate only in passing. The model with no tools stated it outright. (A 2019 result, so there was little literature to find by the cutoff.)

What more compute bought

Seven times the cost: better coverage, same blind spots

Bought

  • The theory and gene-flow substance the light run only gestured at, including the stronger swamping case (Fedorka et al. 2012).
  • Corrections from reading the papers. A famous "no variance at the edge" study selected an interior population; a reported gene-flow asymmetry was not significant.
  • Critics that fixed real errors, with every change and refusal logged.

Not fixed

  • Map edge vs. climate edge is still only in passing (new in 2019, little to find).
  • "No study has done X" stated confidently, and wrong: Sheth & Angert 2016 did it.
  • Its vote count leans on a split it chose after seeing the critics' comments. It said so, which is the right behaviour, and the reason to check.
