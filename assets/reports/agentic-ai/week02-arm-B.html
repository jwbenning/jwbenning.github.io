Do range edges sit where climate changes abruptly?

One agent's test of the idea on 100 North American plants, rewritten from its own code and result files so you can follow both the argument and the machinery.

Analysis: a Claude Opus agent (arm B of the Week 2 experiment), 17 September 2026, working unattended for about 75 minutes of a 96-minute window (a usage cap paused it from 10:20 to 10:40). This write-up: Claude Opus 5.5, 22 September 2026. Every number is B's, read from its data/results/ files. Three of B's claims are corrected here; the last section lists them.

The answer

The idea under test: range edges tend to fall where environmental gradients are non-linear rather than smooth. B split that into two claims, because the sentence contains two, and got a different answer for each.

✓ Supported

Margins sit on steeper precipitation gradients

+19.6%

Median across species: precipitation changes about a fifth faster per km at range margins than at cores. Beats the geometry null (p = 0.003) and survives treating overlapping ranges as one observation. Roughly halves if you drop coastal cells.

~ Not established

The same for temperature, or climate overall

+11.6%

Temperature steepness beats the geometry null (p = 0.010), but its interval crosses zero once species that share a range are collapsed together. The multivariate measure does not beat geometry at all (p = 0.146).

✗ Not supported

Margins are non-linear beyond what steepness predicts

+0.058

Margins are rougher, but by about as much as their steepness implies. The best steepness-adjusted measure is slightly positive for precipitation, but no larger than randomly placed ranges give (null centre +0.033, p = 0.21). Below the null for the other two.

How to read the numbers: each species gets one effect, the log of (median value at margin cells ÷ median at core cells). Zero means no difference; +0.18 means margins are about 20% higher. The headline is the median of those 100 species values. The third card reports a log-scale residual directly, so it has no percentage.

Two questions in one sentence

A species' range is the area where it actually lives. One long-standing idea is that ranges stop where the environment changes too abruptly for populations to keep adapting. "Abruptly" can mean two different things, and B's first move was to separate them in writing, before touching data:

  • Steepness: how fast climate changes per kilometre (the size of the spatial gradient). An even ramp from a wet coast to a dry interior is steep.
  • Non-linearity (B calls it roughness): how badly a straight-line description fails locally. B fits a flat plane to a 5 × 5 block of grid cells and takes the root-mean-square of what's left over. An even ramp, however steep, has zero roughness.

The hypothesis says "non-linear rather than smooth", which is roughness. The instruction B was given says "compare the local spatial rate of change", which is steepness. B measured both. Try the toy below: steepness and roughness move independently, which is what makes the two claims separable.

    iss = step.df["issue"].fillna("")

        bad |= iss.str.contains(f, regex=False).to_numpy()

    # classic sign of a country/state centroid rather than a locality.

    step("integer_degree_centroids", ~both_int)

Climate (WorldClim)

WorldClim 2.1 is a gridded climate product: weather-station averages for 1970–2000, interpolated across the land surface using elevation and satellite data. B used annual mean temperature (bio1), annual precipitation (bio12), and a multivariate summary: the first two principal components of all 19 WorldClim "bioclim" variables, standardised.

Because the analysis takes spatial derivatives, B reprojected everything onto an equal-area grid (Lambert azimuthal, centred at 45°N, 100°W) with 5 km cells. On a latitude/longitude grid a cell is 4.3 km wide at 20°N but 2.3 km wide at 60°N, so "per kilometre" and "a 5 × 5 window" would mean different things in Mexico and in Canada. Resolutions of 2.5′ (primary, 5 km cells), 5′ and 10′ were all run. The 30-arc-second (~1 km) product was not: it needs about 70 GB of disk and the machine had 19 GB free. That is the scale at which a mountain front or a rain-shadow edge is sharpest, so it is the biggest gap in the analysis.

Two grid checks, done off-pipeline. B reports that the equal-area grid's land area matches spherical geometry to 0.27%, and that grid values at ten cities match station normals (mean error 0.20 °C and 28 mm). Both results are typed into the report script rather than computed by the pipeline, so re-running cannot reproduce or fail them. The Week 2 reviewer re-derived both from B's files and they hold. The "published" station values carry no source and look like 1981–2010 or 1991–2020 normals, not WorldClim's 1970–2000.

From points to ranges

"Range margin" is an idea, not a measurement. To compare climate at margins and cores B had to turn scattered points into a shape with an inside and an edge. The primary recipe, applied identically to every species:

  1. Rasterise. A 5 km equal-area cell is occupied if one or more records fall in it.
  2. Close the occupied set with an 80 km disc: grow it outward by 80 km, then shrink it back. Holes smaller than that fill in. Boreal Canada and the Mexican plateau are genuinely occupied but thinly collected, and without this step their ranges shatter.
  3. Drop fragments. Keep the largest connected pieces that together hold 95% of occupied cells. This removes garden plantings and misidentified strays: without it, Trillium grandiflorum, an eastern woodland herb, reached the Pacific coast.
  4. Score depth. For each occupied cell, the distance to the nearest unoccupied cell. Margin = the shallowest 10% of cells; core = the deepest 10%.

The order of steps 2 and 3 matters. B's first version dropped fragments before closing, which cut Pinus banksiana (Alberta to Nova Scotia) down to its well-collected Great Lakes lobe. B caught this by comparing ranges against published range limits, before looking at any climate result, and recorded it in the preregistration.

Closing, fragment filter and depthcode/04_species_grids.py · lines 45–82, 115–131

Morphology is done with distance transforms: grow = cells within r of the range, shrink = cells more than r inside it. components() labels connected pieces (8-neighbour) and keeps the biggest until 95% of cells are covered. depth_of() pads the mask so a range touching the array edge isn't credited with infinite depth. In build_occ, note the order: land mask, then closing, then land mask again (closing can reach into the ocean), then fragments.

def dilate(m, r):
    if r <= 0: return m
    return ndi.distance_transform_edt(~m) <= r


def erode(m, r):
    if r <= 0: return m
    return ndi.distance_transform_edt(m) > r


def closing(m, r):
    if r <= 0: return m
    return erode(dilate(m, r), r)


def components(m, mode):
    if mode == "all" or not m.any():
        return m
    lab, n = ndi.label(m, structure=np.ones((3, 3)))
    if n <= 1:
        return m
    sizes = np.bincount(lab.ravel())[1:]
    if mode == "largest":
        return lab == (int(np.argmax(sizes)) + 1)
    order = np.argsort(sizes)[::-1]          # main95
    keep, cum, tot = [], 0, sizes.sum()
    for oi in order:
        keep.append(int(oi) + 1); cum += sizes[oi]
        if cum >= 0.95 * tot:
            break
    return np.isin(lab, keep)


def depth_of(occ):
    """Distance from each occupied cell to the nearest unoccupied cell.  Padded with False so a
    range abutting the array edge is not credited with infinite depth."""
    p = np.pad(occ, 1, constant_values=False)
    return ndi.distance_transform_edt(p)[1:-1, 1:-1]

def build_occ(cnt_sub, land_sub, thin, min_rec, comp, clo_cells, agg):
    """Occupied-cell mask for one fork combination, inside the species' padded bounding box."""
    if thin == "grid10km" and agg > 1:
        h, w = cnt_sub.shape
        ph, pw = -(-h // agg) * agg, -(-w // agg) * agg
        pad = np.zeros((ph, pw), np.int32); pad[:h, :w] = cnt_sub
        blk = pad.reshape(ph // agg, agg, pw // agg, agg).sum((1, 3))
        occ = np.repeat(np.repeat(blk >= min_rec, agg, 0), agg, 1)[:h, :w]
    else:
        occ = cnt_sub >= min_rec
    occ &= land_sub                      # climate is undefined off the land mask
    if not occ.any():
        return occ
    occ = closing(occ, clo_cells)
    occ &= land_sub
    occ = components(occ, comp)
    return occ

Measuring steepness and roughness

Both are computed for every 5 km land cell from the climate values around it.

  • Steepness: the Horn 3 × 3 operator, a weighted difference between the cells on either side of the focal cell in each direction. Units: °C or mm per km.
  • Roughness: fit a plane z = a + b·x + c·y to the 25 cells of a 5 × 5 window by least squares; take the RMS of the residuals. Units: °C or mm.

Windows that overlap ocean still get a value if at least 60% of the window is land; the fit simply uses the land cells. Requiring complete windows would delete coastal cells, and coastal cells are disproportionately margin cells, so the data most relevant to the hypothesis would go first. Coverage ends up 99.9% for margin cells and 100% for core cells.

Steepness: the Horn operator, made coast-awarecode/surfaces.py · lines 26–58

For each direction it averages three central differences with weights 1, 2, 1. A difference only counts if both of its cells are land, and the weights are renormalised over whatever counts. With a full window this is the textbook Horn (1981) operator.

def horn_gradient(z, mask, cell_km, min_valid=0.6):
    """Horn (1981) 3x3 weighted central difference, generalised to partial windows.

    dz/dx is a weighted mean of the three left-right differences (row weights 1,2,1); a row
    contributes only if BOTH of its flanking cells are land, and the weights are renormalised over
    the contributing rows.  With a complete window this is exactly the standard Horn operator.

    Returns gradient magnitude in <units of z> per km.
    """
    zf = np.where(mask, z, 0.0).astype(np.float64)
    v = mask.astype(np.float64)
    wts = [1.0, 2.0, 1.0]

    num = np.zeros_like(zf); den = np.zeros_like(zf)
    for r, wr in zip((-1, 0, 1), wts):
        zr = _shift(zf, r, 1, 0.0); zl = _shift(zf, r, -1, 0.0)
        vr = _shift(v, r, 1, 0.0);  vl = _shift(v, r, -1, 0.0)
        ok = vr * vl
        num += wr * ok * (zr - zl); den += wr * ok
    dzdx = np.divide(num, den * 2.0 * cell_km, out=np.full_like(num, np.nan), where=den > 0)

    num = np.zeros_like(zf); den = np.zeros_like(zf)
    for csh, wc in zip((-1, 0, 1), wts):
        zd = _shift(zf, 1, csh, 0.0); zu = _shift(zf, -1, csh, 0.0)
        vd = _shift(v, 1, csh, 0.0);  vu = _shift(v, -1, csh, 0.0)
        ok = vd * vu
        num += wc * ok * (zd - zu); den += wc * ok
    dzdy = np.divide(num, den * 2.0 * cell_km, out=np.full_like(num, np.nan), where=den > 0)

    vf = correlate(v, np.ones((3, 3)), mode="constant", cval=0.0) / 9.0
    g = np.hypot(dzdx, dzdy)
    g[(vf < min_valid) | ~mask] = np.nan
    return g, vf
Roughness: a least-squares plane in every window at oncecode/surfaces.py · lines 61–124

Rather than looping over ~900,000 windows, it builds the sums a plane fit needs (Σ1, Σx, Σxz, …) for every cell with one convolution each, then solves all the 3 × 3 normal equations in batches. The residual sum of squares is Σz² − β·r, so roughness never needs the individual residuals. Ocean cells get weight zero, which is how partial coastal windows work.

def planar_fit(z, mask, k, cell_km, min_valid=0.6, components=False):
    """Weighted least-squares fit of a PLANE  z ~ a + b*x + c*y  over each k x k window,
    using only the land cells in that window.

    Returns
      slope_km : sqrt(b^2+c^2) converted to <units of z> per km   -- the 'planar' gradient fork
      rms      : root-mean-square residual from the fitted plane, in units of z
                 -- 'how badly does a flat plane describe this neighbourhood', i.e. the
                    departure-from-linearity that the hypothesis is actually about
      vf       : valid fraction of the window

    Note: the residual RMS is invariant to any invertible linear rescaling of the (x,y)
    coordinates, so it is identical whether x,y are measured in cells or in km.  Only the SLOPE
    needs the metric conversion.  This is what lets the same routine serve the native-lat/lon fork.
    """
    c0 = k // 2
    q = np.arange(k) - c0
    KX = np.tile(q, (k, 1)).astype(np.float64)          # column offset (x)
    KY = KX.T.copy()                                    # row offset (y)
    ones = np.ones((k, k))

    v = mask.astype(np.float64)
    zv = np.where(mask, z, 0.0).astype(np.float64)

    def cor(a, K):
        return correlate(a, K, mode="constant", cval=0.0)

    S0 = cor(v, ones); Sx = cor(v, KX); Sy = cor(v, KY)
    Sxx = cor(v, KX * KX); Syy = cor(v, KY * KY); Sxy = cor(v, KX * KY)
    Sz = cor(zv, ones); Szx = cor(zv, KX); Szy = cor(zv, KY); Szz = cor(zv * zv, ones)

    vf = S0 / (k * k)
    good = (vf >= min_valid) & mask & (S0 >= 3)
    idx = np.flatnonzero(good.ravel())

    slope = np.full(z.shape, np.nan, dtype=np.float64)
    rms = np.full(z.shape, np.nan, dtype=np.float64)
    bx = np.full(z.shape, np.nan, dtype=np.float64)
    by = np.full(z.shape, np.nan, dtype=np.float64)

    flat = [a.ravel() for a in (S0, Sx, Sy, Sxx, Syy, Sxy, Sz, Szx, Szy, Szz)]
    CH = 400_000
    for s in range(0, idx.size, CH):
        ii = idx[s:s + CH]
        s0, sx, sy, sxx, syy, sxy, sz, szx, szy, szz = (a[ii] for a in flat)
        M = np.empty((ii.size, 3, 3))
        M[:, 0, 0] = s0;  M[:, 0, 1] = sx;  M[:, 0, 2] = sy
        M[:, 1, 0] = sx;  M[:, 1, 1] = sxx; M[:, 1, 2] = sxy
        M[:, 2, 0] = sy;  M[:, 2, 1] = sxy; M[:, 2, 2] = syy
        r = np.stack([sz, szx, szy], axis=1)
        det = np.linalg.det(M)
        okd = np.abs(det) > 1e-8 * (np.abs(s0) ** 3 + 1.0)
        beta = np.full((ii.size, 3), np.nan)
        if okd.any():
            # numpy>=2 treats a trailing (N,3) as a matrix, not a batch of vectors
            beta[okd] = np.linalg.solve(M[okd], r[okd][:, :, None])[:, :, 0]
        rss = szz - np.einsum("ij,ij->i", beta, r)
        slope.ravel()[ii] = np.hypot(beta[:, 1], beta[:, 2]) / cell_km
        rms.ravel()[ii] = np.sqrt(np.maximum(rss, 0.0) / s0)
        bx.ravel()[ii] = beta[:, 1]     # d z / d(column), units of z per cell
        by.ravel()[ii] = beta[:, 2]     # d z / d(row),    units of z per cell
    if components:
        return slope, rms, vf, bx, by
    return slope, rms, vf

Before running them on climate, B checked the estimators on surfaces with known answers. A plane z = 3 + 0.5x − 0.2y returns the exact gradient (error 2 × 10⁻¹⁶) and zero roughness; a steep plane and a gentle plane both return roughness 0.00000; a plane plus white noise (sd 1) returns 0.92 against an expected ~0.94; masking 30% of cells at random costs a 1.3% median error.

How the comparison is made

For each species: take the median steepness (or roughness) over its margin cells and over its core cells, and form the log ratio. Then ask whether the 100 species values are centred above zero, with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a bootstrap interval on the median (resampling species).

The unit of analysis is the species, not the grid cell. Neighbouring cells have nearly the same climate, so treating ~600,000 margin cells and ~590,000 core cells as independent gives a p-value that reflects sample size. For precipitation steepness the pooled-cell test returns p = 0 (it underflows); the species-level test gives p = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵. B computed the pooled version only to show the size of the inflation.

Margin and core: the outer and inner 10% of depthcode/analysis_lib.py · lines 18–32
def classify(score, q, tail):
    """Return boolean masks (margin, core) for the outer/inner q of a marginality score.

    Ties matter here and are not hidden.  `depth` is a Euclidean distance transform, so a large
    block of cells sits at exactly 1.0 (every cell touching the range edge).  Taking
    `score <= quantile(score, q)` therefore returns MORE than a fraction q of cells whenever the
    q-th quantile falls on a tie -- for a geometrically thin range it can be a third of them.
    We do not break the tie: a cell one step from the edge is not more marginal than its
    neighbour just because of its array index.  The realised fractions are recorded per species
    and reported, and the equal-n rank alternative is run as a fork.
    """
    lo, hi = np.quantile(score, q), np.quantile(score, 1 - q)
    if tail == "low":
        return score <= lo, score >= hi
    return score >= hi, score <= lo
One number per speciescode/analysis_lib.py · lines 63–92

The residual measure is already on a log scale and can be negative, so it is compared by difference rather than by log ratio; B's first version used the ratio for it too, which silently dropped half the species, and B found and fixed this before reading results.

def effect(vm, vc, stat, already_log=False):
    """Per-species margin-vs-core effect.

    'median'/'mean'  -> log ratio, so that +0.10 means the margin value is ~10.5% higher and the
                        statistic is symmetric under swapping the two groups.
    'cliffs_delta'   -> rank-based, bounded [-1,1], no distributional assumption, and insensitive
                        to the heavy right tail that both gradient and roughness have.

    `already_log=True` is required for metrics that are THEMSELVES residuals on a log scale
    (normrough5_resid).  Such a metric is centred on zero and is negative for roughly half of all
    cells, so log(median_margin / median_core) is undefined whenever either median is negative.
    Silently dropping those species is not a missing-data problem, it is a selection effect: the
    survivors are exactly the species whose margins AND cores are both rougher than the landscape
    regression predicts.  For these metrics the effect is the DIFFERENCE of medians, which is
    already a log ratio because the underlying quantity is a log.
    """
    vm = vm[np.isfinite(vm)]; vc = vc[np.isfinite(vc)]
    if vm.size < 20 or vc.size < 20:
        return np.nan, np.nan, np.nan
    if stat == "cliffs_delta":
        return cliffs_delta(vm, vc), np.median(vm), np.median(vc)
    f = np.median if stat == "median" else np.mean
    a, b = float(f(vm)), float(f(vc))
    if not (np.isfinite(a) and np.isfinite(b)):
        return np.nan, a, b
    if already_log:
        return a - b, a, b
    if a <= 0 or b <= 0:
        return np.nan, a, b
    return float(np.log(a / b)), a, b
Across species: bootstrap interval and Wilcoxon testcode/analysis_lib.py · lines 95–119
def across_species(lrs, n_boot=10000, seed=1):
    """Inference with the SPECIES as the unit of analysis.

    Grid cells inside one range are strongly spatially autocorrelated; pooling ~10^5 of them gives a
    p-value that measures sample size, not nature.  So each species contributes exactly one number
    and we ask whether that distribution is centred away from zero.
    """
    x = np.asarray([v for v in lrs if np.isfinite(v)], float)
    out = {"n_species": int(x.size)}
    if x.size < 3:
        return {**out, "median": np.nan, "mean": np.nan, "ci_lo": np.nan, "ci_hi": np.nan,
                "wilcoxon_p": np.nan, "frac_positive": np.nan, "sign_p": np.nan}
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    boot = np.median(x[rng.integers(0, x.size, (n_boot, x.size))], axis=1)
    try:
        wp = float(stats.wilcoxon(x, alternative="two-sided").pvalue)
    except Exception:
        wp = np.nan
    npos = int((x > 0).sum())
    return {**out,
            "median": float(np.median(x)), "mean": float(np.mean(x)),
            "ci_lo": float(np.percentile(boot, 2.5)), "ci_hi": float(np.percentile(boot, 97.5)),
            "wilcoxon_p": wp,
            "frac_positive": npos / x.size,
            "sign_p": float(stats.binomtest(npos, x.size, 0.5).pvalue)}
Why the interval and the p-value sometimes disagree. For temperature roughness the bootstrap interval on the median clears zero [+0.044, +0.204] while the Wilcoxon p is 0.066. They test different things: the interval is about the median, and the Wilcoxon test is about the whole distribution's centre of symmetry. The species distributions have heavy left tails (temperature steepness: median +0.109, mean +0.059, seven species below −0.5), which pulls the Wilcoxon centre down. Primary specification, all measures

The geometry null

North America has coasts on three sides and mountains down the west. The edge of any compact shape dropped on it will tend to be nearer a coast or a mountain front than its middle is. If arbitrary shapes have steeper edges than centres, real range edges will too, and that tells us nothing about range limits.

So B took each species' real range, rotated it by a random angle, dropped it somewhere else (at least 90% on land), redrew margin and core with the identical rule, and recomputed the effect. Range size, shape and the margin rule are preserved, as is the real climate field; the species' own position is not. 999 draws per species, 36 rotation angles.

The null is centred well above zero: +0.050 for precipitation steepness, +0.049 for the multivariate measure. Arbitrary blobs do have steeper edges than middles. Without this null a large chunk of pure geometry would have been reported as biology. A second, weaker null (shuffling margin and core labels within a species) only tests sampling noise; B said in advance that beating it would not count as a result.

Null, part 1: rotate each range and pre-compute its margincode/07_nulls.py · lines 53–66

Depth depends only on the shape, not on where it sits, so margin and core are computed once per rotation (36 angles) and reused for every placement. That trick is what makes 999 draws × 100 species × 12 measures run in about 25 minutes.

def rotations(occ_sub, q, n_angles, rng):
    """Pre-rotate a species mask and pre-classify margin/core for each angle."""
    out = []
    for ang in np.linspace(0, 360, n_angles, endpoint=False):
        rot = occ_sub if ang == 0 else ndi.rotate(occ_sub, ang, order=0, reshape=True,
                                                  prefilter=False, mode="constant", cval=False)
        rot = rot.astype(bool)
        if rot.sum() < C.MIN_OCCUPIED_CELLS:
            continue
        rr, cc = np.nonzero(rot)
        dep = depth_of(rot)[rr, cc].astype(np.float64)
        mm, co = classify(dep, q, "low")
        out.append({"shape": rot.shape, "r": rr, "c": cc, "m": mm, "k": co})
    return out

Roughness given steepness

Raw roughness is higher at margins, but that is not independent evidence. Across the continent, rough places are steep places: over 868,000 land cells the correlation between log steepness and log roughness is 0.78 to 0.81 depending on the variable. So the real test of "non-linear rather than smooth" is whether margins are rougher than their steepness predicts. B adjusted in two ways, and they do not behave the same.

Precipitation: steepness against roughness, 2,500 random land cells
Fitted line, slope (what the residual removes) Slope 1 (what the ratio assumes)

Both axes log₁₀. The ratio treats roughness as proportional to steepness, so it divides out a slope-1 line. The data rise more slowly than that, so the ratio punishes steep cells: log(roughness ÷ steepness) correlates −0.31, −0.38 and −0.28 with log steepness for temperature, precipitation and multivariate. The residual from the fitted line is uncorrelated with steepness by construction (r = 0.000).

Real margins are steeper than the margins of randomly placed blobs, so the ratio pushes real margins down relative to the null for mechanical reasons. That is why every ratio result sits below its null. The residual is the fair adjustment, and it says:

  • Precipitation: +0.058, positive in 57% of species, with the null centred at +0.033. Above the null, but not significantly (p = 0.21). Positive under 20 of 21 specifications.
  • Temperature and multivariate: −0.024 and −0.012, below null centres of +0.040 and +0.030.

The conclusion holds: no variable shows margins that are rougher than their steepness predicts by more than geometry does. But B's report stated three times that every steepness-adjusted measure sits below the null, which is only true of the ratio. The precipitation residual rows, which contradict it, never appeared in B's null table or its summary table, because both were built from a hard-coded list of measures:

The residual itself is computed like this, once per variable across all land cells:

The ratio and the residual, side by sidecode/06_main_analysis.py · lines 70–94

The ratio divides roughness by steepness × cell size. The residual fits log(roughness) = a + b·log(steepness) across all land cells and keeps what is left, so the adjustment uses the slope the data actually have (0.75 for precipitation) rather than assuming 1.

def measure(self, var, metric):
    """Return the 2-D metric raster, deriving the normalised-roughness variants on demand."""
    if metric in ("grad_horn3", "grad_planar5", "rough5", "rough7", "rough9", "lap"):
        return self.layer(f"{var}__{metric}")
    g = self.layer(f"{var}__grad_horn3"); r = self.layer(f"{var}__rough5")
    ck = self.cell_km if np.isfinite(self.cell_km) else 1.0
    if metric == "normrough5_ratio":
        den = g * ck
        out = np.full(g.shape, np.nan, np.float32)
        ok = np.isfinite(g) & np.isfinite(r) & (den > 0)
        out[ok] = r[ok] / den[ok]
        return out
    # normrough5_resid: regress log(rough) on log(grad) over ALL land cells, keep residuals
    key = f"__resid__{var}"
    if key in self._cache:
        return self._cache[key]
    ok = np.isfinite(g) & np.isfinite(r) & (g > 0) & (r > 0)
    X = np.log(g[ok]); Y = np.log(r[ok])
    A = np.vstack([np.ones_like(X), X]).T
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, Y, rcond=None)
    out = np.full(g.shape, np.nan, np.float32)
    out[ok] = Y - (coef[0] + coef[1] * X)
    self._cache[key] = out
    self._cache[key + "__coef"] = coef
    return out

Every fork, one at a time

B was told to treat each analytical choice as a fork and run the alternatives. It did this one at a time: the primary specification, plus 20 versions each changing exactly one choice. A full cross of every option would have been thousands of runs, mostly measuring interactions nobody could interpret.

Median across species with its 95% bootstrap interval, one row per specification. These intervals resample species, so they are narrower than the cluster intervals that decide the verdict. Cliff's delta is a rank statistic on a different scale (−1 to 1), so compare its sign, not its size. Hover a row for details.

Four forks move the answer, and each has a reason unrelated to the outcome:

  • No closing collapses every effect to about zero. It also fails the independent range check (Jaccard 0.45 against 0.82): without gap-filling, thinly collected ranges shatter and the fragment filter deletes most of them.
  • Margin = sparsest records reverses the sign. Calling the least-collected cells the "margin" measures collecting effort, not range position, which is why B set it up as a demonstration of the confound rather than a candidate primary.
  • Margin = climatically extreme gives the largest effects, but is close to circular: cells far from a species' climatic average are partly selected for sitting where climate changes fast.
  • Mean instead of median roughly doubles the steepness effect, because steepness has a long right tail.

Resolution, resampling method, reprojection, fragment filtering, the 10/20/25% cut, and equal-sized classes leave the picture as the primary shows it. Two forks B preregistered were never run: a coordinate-uncertainty filter and a post-1950 date filter. Both are defined in config.py and used nowhere, and the report does not say so.

What could still be wrong

The species are not 100 independent observations

Many eastern woodland herbs in the list share nearly the same range, so they share margin cells and climate. Mean pairwise overlap (Jaccard) is 0.12, the maximum 0.78, and 21% of pairs overlap by more than a quarter. Clustering species that overlap by more than 25% leaves 17 groups, the two largest holding 34 and 24 species. Bootstrapping over clusters instead of species widens every interval, and it is this correction that sinks temperature steepness: [+0.056, +0.178] over species becomes [−0.042, +0.173] over clusters.

Coastlines

A coast is a break in every climate variable at once, and in the interpolation that made the grid. Deleting every cell within 100 km of the coast roughly halves the precipitation effect (+0.179 to +0.094) and turns multivariate roughness negative. The explorer's "inland only" readout shows this for each measure. A coast is a climate discontinuity, an interpolation edge and a place plants cannot live, and this analysis cannot separate the three.

Collecting effort

Margin cells sit where pooled collecting effort is 34% lower than at core cells. Species whose margins are most under-collected relative to their cores do not show larger effects (all correlations near zero, none significant), which rules out the simplest version of the confound. The shared-cause version remains: WorldClim looks more detailed where weather stations are dense, and station density tracks people, who also produce GBIF records.

A second implementation, written to disagree

B recomputed the primary number on 25 species through a separate code path sharing no functions with the main one: Albers instead of Lambert projection, climate read straight from the original files, numpy.gradient instead of the Horn operator, erosion instead of a distance transform, and "touching an unoccupied cell" as the margin. Medians agree to within 0.009 log units (temperature) and 0.001 (precipitation), rank correlations 0.71 and 0.76. An earlier 12-species trial of the same check showed a large temperature disagreement that disappeared at 25; small-n agreement statistics are noisy too.

Smaller things

  • "The shallowest 10%" is not exactly 10%. Depth ties at the range edge, so the margin class holds a median 10.5% of cells (up to 12.3%). The equal-sized-classes fork removes this and changes nothing.
  • About 0.4% of occupied cells fall outside any checklist-native region. Strays form their own edge, so they land in the margin class.
  • No literature is cited anywhere in B's report.

What survives what

Each column is a test a result has to pass. The raw effect is what a naive analysis would report; the next columns strip out range geometry, shared ranges, and coasts in turn.

Beats geometry: one-sided p < 0.05 against the displacement null. Cluster interval: 95% bootstrap over the 17 range clusters. Supported requires both. Inland: the median effect after deleting cells within 100 km of the coast, shown for context rather than as a pass/fail test.

What it means

The defensible claim is narrow: for annual precipitation, in these 100 species, range margins sit where precipitation changes about 20% faster per kilometre than at range cores, by more than range geometry produces. It rests on about 17 independent range clusters rather than 100 species, about half of it disappears when coastal cells are removed, and some unknown share reflects where climate is measured best. The literal hypothesis, that edges sit on non-linear rather than merely steep climate, is not supported at any resolution this data reaches.

The steepness result is the one that bears on range-limit theory. Models of adaptation along an environmental gradient (Kirkpatrick & Barton 1997; Polechová & Barton 2015) predict that a stable margin forms where the gradient becomes too steep for selection to keep up with gene flow. That is a claim about steepness, and B's steepness result is consistent with it. Two cautions: steepness here is measured over 15 km windows, which is local slope rather than a landscape-scale steepening, and nothing in this analysis separates "steep climate stops a species" from "species stop for other reasons near topography, where climate is also steep."

What would change the answer. The 30-arc-second WorldClim grid on a machine with ~100 GB free: roughness is scale-dependent, and 1 km is where climate transitions are sharpest. Second, a species set with genuinely independent ranges, since 17 clusters is the binding limit on every interval here.

Where this differs from B's own report

Same analysis, same numbers. These are the substantive changes:

  • The residual measure is shown everywhere. B's null and summary tables left out the regression-residual rows, and its text said every steepness-adjusted measure sits below the geometric null. For precipitation the residual sits above its null centre (+0.058 against +0.033), though not significantly. The conclusion stands; the stated reason does not.
  • The ratio is labelled as biased. B used roughness ÷ steepness as its headline adjusted measure and described its below-null values as margins being "slightly less non-linear than randomly placed blobs". That is an artefact of dividing by steepness when roughness grows more slowly than steepness (section 9).
  • Two undisclosed gaps are disclosed: the grid checks are hard-coded in the report script, and two preregistered forks (coordinate uncertainty, post-1950 records) were never run.
  • The transect figure uses a fixed axis. B's version auto-scaled each panel, so its "steep" and "gentle" ramps looked the same.
  • The 504-row appendix table is replaced by the fork explorer, whose rows are distinguishable; in B's table, three roughness windows shared one label.
